Making financial reporting easier to verify

Two directions, both built on audit experience. I'll pick one to build after talking to users, before November 10.

Auditors at small firms spend days hand-building reconciliations from client journals before they can start testing. Audit Workbench turns raw journals into reconciled workpapers and a flagged list of what to look into.

The problem

Every line item on the financial statements is audited differently. Each has its own movements, its own supporting schedules, and its own column layouts: a cash book looks nothing like an inventory movement report. For each one, the auditor rebuilds the balance from last year's closing figure, checks every addition and deduction against its source, and judges whether the movements make sense.

Opening AR + Credit sales ← sales register − Cash receipts ← cash receipts register − Credit notes & returns ← credit note register − Write-offs ← write-off listing = Closing AR ← aged AR listing

This is slow, manual, and repeated on every engagement. Large vendors (MindBridge, Caseware, Fieldguide, DataSnipper) are priced for big firms, and the Big 4 build their own platforms. Small firms are left with spreadsheets.

Who it's for

Auditors at small external audit firms and sole practitioners working under IFRS and ISA.

The ambitious version

Upload a client's journals and supporting schedules. The tool maps accounts to line items, rebuilds every statement and reconciliation, compares them against the statements the client prepared, and flags anything that doesn't tie or doesn't make sense across every line item. It hands back a full Excel workpaper set and a short summary of where to look deeper, with each flag linked to the relevant audit procedure and accounting standard.

How v1 works: Revenue and receivables

  1. Upload the journal entries, five supporting schedules in fixed templates, last year's closing trial balance, and the client's own trial balance.
  2. Map accounts. The AI suggests which client accounts belong to Revenue and AR, and the auditor confirms.
  3. Rebuild the lead schedule and the AR reconciliation shown above.
  4. Compare the rebuilt figures against the client's trial balance and each schedule, and flag every difference.
  5. Flag the unusual:
    • big month-over-month and year-over-year swings;
    • suspicious entries: revenue booked against accounts other than receivables or cash, manual entries in the last days of the year, round amounts, weekend postings;
    • movements that don't make sense: returns rate, days sales outstanding, sales just before year-end vs. shipping dates, cash collected after year-end.
  6. Download an Excel workpaper and read a short summary. The AI only describes flags the rules found; it never calculates numbers.

Each flag links to the procedures to perform next, from a library I write myself. It cites IFRS and ISA paragraphs and paraphrases them; their text isn't reproduced.

Testing without confidential data

Firms can't share client data with a student project, so I generate synthetic companies. A double-entry engine posts balanced transactions, so every schedule ties out. Then it plants known misstatements and records them in an answer key. This lets me measure exactly what the tool catches and what it misses.

Focus: AI AI

The valuable parts are judgment calls an AI model can help with, and synthetic companies give me a correct answer to measure them against:

  • Account mapping: how often does the AI map accounts correctly?
  • Detection: of the misstatements I planted, how many get flagged, and how many false flags appear?
  • Summary faithfulness: does every claim trace back to a real flag, and every citation to my procedure library?

How I'll know it works

  • Eval scores on mapping, detection, and false flags across a set of synthetic companies.
  • Three auditors work through a synthetic case and tell me which flags they would actually follow up on, and how long the same work takes by hand.

People researching a stock see ratios everywhere, but never see when a company quietly changed its past numbers. Filing Analyzer explains a company's financial health and surfaces every revised value in its SEC filings, explained the way an auditor would.

The problem

Ratios and red-flag scores are everywhere: Stock Analysis shows Piotroski and Altman Z for free, and GuruFocus shows the Beneish M-Score. But no consumer tool shows when a number a company already reported was later changed.

The SEC's free data records each value for every filing that reported it, so the same year can be compared across filings. For example, Apple's fiscal 2009 total assets were $53.85B in its original 10-K and $47.50B in the amended 10-K/A. Most restatements are quiet revisions like this, not headline events.

Who it's for

People investing their own money who research a US public company before buying, and finance and accounting students.

How it works

  1. Enter a ticker for any US company that files with the SEC.
  2. Health overview: key ratios and red-flag scores, explained in plain language from an auditor's point of view.
  3. Revised values: a timeline of every number that changed between filings. Each change has a cause and links to both filings:
    • split adjustment: per-share numbers changed by a stock split;
    • reclassification: amounts moved between line items;
    • standard adoption: a new accounting rule applied retroactively;
    • correction: an error in a past filing was fixed.
  4. Summary: a short AI-written status with a citation for every number.
  5. Watchlist of companies you follow.

Simple rules catch the obvious causes first. For example, a change that exactly matches a split ratio is a split. The AI then classifies what's left as reclassification or correction.

Focus: AI AI

Deciding why a number changed is a real judgment call, and its answers can be checked. I'll hand-label about 50 real revised values and measure how often the AI gets the cause right. I'll also check that every number in the summary matches the SEC data it cites.

How I'll know it works

  • Classification accuracy on my hand-labeled set.
  • Users find a revised value in a company they follow that they didn't know about, and understand why it changed.
